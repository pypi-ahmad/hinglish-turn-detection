Lesson 8

Defend the choices, including the weak ones

A strong explanation separates what you built, what you measured, and what you still do not know.

Your two-minute explanation

I built an audio turn-completion classifier for Hindi and English speech. It uses the Whisper Tiny encoder, keeps the final eight seconds, pools encoder frames, and predicts the probability that a turn is complete. I focused on fillers and pauses through label-preserving augmentation and hard-example sampling.

I compared data policy, pooling, silence ranges, encoder freezing, and an audio-plus-text model. Aggregate F1 was not enough because a false complete interrupts the user. A three-seed safety study selected a last-frame model using validation-only FCR and recall constraints. Its held-out FCR was 9.84 percent, though recall fell to 83.26 percent.

The largest limitation is data. The source has no speaker IDs, transcripts, or verified Hinglish labels, so Hindi and filler results are proxies. The next useful step is consented, speaker-disjoint human Hinglish data, not another small classifier change.

Questions you should answer aloud

Why Whisper Tiny?

It is small and already has multilingual acoustic representations. Keeping only the encoder holds the classifier near 7.9 million parameters.

Why last-frame pooling?

It was not chosen by intuition alone. E4 performed well in the controlled study and won median constrained validation F1 across three seeds.

Why not claim Hinglish accuracy?

The source lacks verified code-switch transcripts. Hindi tags and filler metadata are useful proxy slices, but they cannot establish a true Hinglish benchmark.

What would you improve first?

Collect human conversations with consent, speaker IDs, transcripts, filler and pause annotations, then create a fixed speaker-disjoint challenge set.
